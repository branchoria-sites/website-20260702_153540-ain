## Introduction

[Creuse]({{'what-do-creuse-ufo-reports-really-show/' | relative_url }})’s GEIPAN record is thin, but that is exactly why it is useful. The French space agency’s public UFO archive does not show a department crowded with dramatic unresolved cases. It shows a small set of reports in which the decisive issue is usually evidence quality: who saw the object, whether there was a photograph, whether the timing could be checked, and whether later investigation could test the claim. In the current public case list, Creuse includes a handful of files ranging from a 1976 case classed as probably identified, to a 1988 Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois case classed as category C because the information is insufficient, to later cases explained as astronomical scintillation, an aircraft, and a gas balloon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=133&amp;sort=desc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for GEIPAN Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

## What GEIPAN Records for Creuse

GEIPAN is the French official body, within CNES, that collects, analyses, archives and publishes reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena. CNES states that the programme began in 1977 and works with partners including the gendarmerie, police, air and space force, CNRS and Météo-France. Its published figures also show why caution is built into the system: only a small minority of all cases are classed as unidentified after investigation, while a much larger share are either identified, probably identified, or left without enough data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/projets/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPAN &#124; CNES</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPAN &#124; CNES</span></span></span>

For Creuse, the public record is modest. GEIPAN’s case-search results list five department entries: La Courtine in 1976, Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois in 1988, a department-wide Creuse astronomical case in 1989, Sermur in 2018, and the N145 observation from Saint-Priest-la-Plaine towards La Souterraine in 2024. Their classifications are mixed but telling: one B case, one C case, and three A cases. In plain terms, that means the public Creuse archive contains one probably identified file, one file that cannot be properly analysed, and several files that GEIPAN considers identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=133&amp;sort=desc&amp;undefined=" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The newer identified cases help set the tone. At Sermur in August 2018, a witness photographed what appeared to be five aligned lights and a rectangular flat structure; GEIPAN concluded that the repeated red, white and green light pattern, the six-second exposure, and the apparent motion were consistent with a passenger aircraft. The case was classed A, with GEIPAN noting that the image supported the explanation even though the exact aircraft could not be formally identified because of the delay before processing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2018-08-50680" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The 2024 N145 case shows an even more modern kind of evidence check. A driver photographed a grey point in the sky near Saint-Priest-la-Plaine on 15 September 2024. GEIPAN identified it as a gas balloon from the 2024 Gordon Bennett Cup, using the photograph, the witness location, the direction of observation, and balloon tracking data. The apparent stillness and sudden disappearance were explained by distance, the witness’s own motion, lighting conditions, and the balloon’s position.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2024-09-51600" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geipan[N145] DE SAINT-PRIEST-LA-PLAINE (23) VERS</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan[N145] DE SAINT-PRIEST-LA-PLAINE (23) VERS</span></span></span>

The 1989 Creuse case is also important because it looks like a small local cluster at first glance. GEIPAN describes multiple gendarmerie brigades observing bright, scintillating points in the eastern sky over several evenings, then explains the phenomenon as astronomical: star scintillation under favourable weather conditions. That case is classed A, not because nobody was puzzled, but because the pattern across brigades was explainable once astronomy and viewing conditions were considered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1989-01-01165" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="GEIPAN Files illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## The Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois Problem

The file that creates the main evidence gap is Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois, observed on 13 October 1988. GEIPAN’s summary describes a yellow luminous ball, roughly the size of a car headlamp, seen for about half an hour at around 20:30. It reportedly moved back and forth at high speed, both horizontally and vertically, without any detected noise. The witness’s spouse also saw the phenomenon, but GEIPAN’s published case page ends with the crucial limitation: no other information could be gathered. The case is classed C, with the phenomenon type given as lack of reliable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-10-01150" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The gendarmerie file adds useful texture, but not enough to resolve the case. The report says the gendarmes learned of the observation two days later through local rumour, visited the site, and recorded that the witnesses had not called the gendarmerie on the evening itself because they feared they would not be taken seriously. The observation point was given as 352 metres above sea level; the sky was described as completely clear, the night dark, visibility good, and the wind almost absent. The report also says the Moon was full around that period but positioned further east than the observed object, and that no obstacle lay in the field of view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0528%20%281988307092%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

There are several details that make the testimony interesting. The main witness said the object was circular, bright yellow, at a height she could not judge, and moving faster than an aircraft in repeated vertical and horizontal motions. She added that no beam came from it, that it sometimes appeared stationary, and that she noticed a slight yellowish trail beneath it at one point. Her husband’s statement was shorter: he saw a circular luminous object, flame-coloured, moving quickly without noise, but only watched for a few moments before going to bed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0528%20%281988307092%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The file also contains the beginnings of a check for alternative witnesses and obvious official [explanations]({{'explanations/' | relative_url }}). Gendarmes contacted people in the surrounding area and found no other witness. A fishmonger’s lorry, which might have provided an independent sighting because it usually passed at that hour, had not passed through on the evening in question. The file also states that no military air or ground manoeuvre had been reported in the region on 13 October 1988.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0528%20%281988307092%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Those points matter, but they do not close the gap. The case has two related witnesses in the same household, no photograph, no radar or air-traffic correlation in the published file, no independent local corroboration, no precise angular measurements, no exact direction beyond the broad north-east indication, and no documented reconstruction that would allow modern readers to test aircraft, celestial, balloon, vehicle-light or atmospheric explanations with confidence. The report is valuable as a historical witness record, but it is not a strong evidential platform.<div class="youtube-embed-container youtube-embed-fallback"><div class="youtube-embed-card"><div class="youtube-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/zczcBLukQ6s" title="Meeting France&#x27;s UFO detectives • FRANCE 24 English" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><div class="youtube-embed-footer"><p class="youtube-embed-title"><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zczcBLukQ6s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Meeting France&#x27;s UFO detectives • FRANCE 24 English</a></p><p class="youtube-embed-meta">Channel: FRANCE 24 English &middot; Views: 48.3K &middot; Uploaded: October 2024 &middot; Length: 11 minutes</p><p class="youtube-embed-actions"><a class="youtube-embed-watch-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zczcBLukQ6s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" title="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zczcBLukQ6s">Open on YouTube</a></p></div></div></div>

## How Category C Changes the Claim

GEIPAN’s classification system is designed to prevent exactly the mistake that often happens in UFO retellings: treating “not explained” as if it automatically meant “strong mystery”. GEIPAN says its classification rests on two ideas: residual strangeness after investigation, and consistency, meaning the quantity and reliability of the data collected. Category A means identified after investigation; B means probably identified; C means not identified because of a lack of data or information; D means not identified after investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geipan Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

That distinction is central to Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois. A category D case would imply that investigators had enough reliable material to examine the report and still could not explain it. Category C says something different. It says the case cannot support a firm conclusion because the evidence is too thin or not workable. GEIPAN’s own methodology says that if the relationship between strangeness and consistency is not strong enough, the observation becomes a C case: impossible to conclude from, rather than an unexplained case of high evidential weight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Geipan La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Geipan La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

For readers, this changes the honest wording of the claim. It is fair to say that two people in Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois reported a luminous object moving unusually on 13 October 1988, and that gendarmes recorded their account shortly afterwards. It is also fair to say the public GEIPAN file contains no published explanation. But it is not fair to present the case as a robust official mystery. GEIPAN’s classification tells us that the missing evidence is part of the result.

This is especially important in a rural department such as Creuse. INSEE described Creuse in 2018 as the second least populated and least dense department in metropolitan France, and its more recent estimate gives the department a population of about 115,000 in the mid-2020s. Fewer nearby observers, fewer overlapping reports, and fewer immediate technical traces can make a striking account harder to test after the fact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/5763691" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insee.fr">[Insee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insee.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on insee.fr.</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="GEIPAN Files illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/Creuse_73fb1d_geipan_creuse_files_cd11f1-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Why the Thin Record Still Matters

A thin official file can matter in two opposite ways. It can preserve a report that might otherwise survive only as local rumour, and it can stop that rumour from being inflated beyond the evidence. The Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois file does both. It records the human story: a witness watching from a farm courtyard, calling her husband, seeing a silent light that seemed too fast and too strange to identify. It also records the evidential limits: delayed reporting, no independent witnesses, no confirmed military activity, and no follow-up evidence capable of settling the cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/sites/default/files/PV%20n%C2%B0528%20%281988307092%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan+2Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The contrast with Sermur and the N145 case is useful. In both modern examples, GEIPAN had a photograph. At Sermur, the photograph’s light pattern and exposure time were enough to support an aircraft explanation. In the N145 case, a photograph plus position and external tracking data led to a specific balloon explanation. These are not spectacular debunkings; they are examples of how a modest extra piece of evidence can move a case from “puzzling account” to “identified phenomenon”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2018-08-50680" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Reading Creuse’s GEIPAN Files Responsibly

The safest reading of Creuse’s official UFO history is that the department has a small but instructive record, not a hidden catalogue of confirmed anomalies. The strongest public lesson is methodological. Some reports become explainable because photographs, timing, direction, astronomical context or tracking data give investigators something to test. Others remain unresolved in the everyday sense because they are too thin to analyse, not because they have survived a demanding investigation.

Saint-Sulpice-le-Dunois should therefore sit at the centre of the Creuse evidence-gap story. It is the file that most resembles a classic rural UFO account: a silent light, unusual movement, night-time setting, sincere witnesses, and a gendarmerie record. But the same file shows why sincerity is not the same as proof. The official paper trail preserves the account while refusing to make it stronger than the data allow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-10-01150" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan+2Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

For Creuse as a department-level UFO subject, that is a useful conclusion rather than a disappointing one. The record does not ask readers to choose between belief and dismissal. It asks them to separate three categories that often get blurred together: reports that are identified, reports that are probably identified, and reports that remain thin because the evidence needed to decide has never been recovered.
