## Introduction

[Allier]({{'what-alliers-ufo-files-really-show/' | relative_url }})’s explained UFO record is less a trail of hidden mysteries than a practical lesson in how strange-looking lights become ordinary once timing, direction, photographs, weather, astronomy and aircraft activity are checked. In the public GEIPAN/CNES record, the department includes many cases classed as A, meaning identified, and one notable B case, meaning a very probable explanation; the independent CarteOvni index, which republishes public GEIPAN data, lists Allier with 18 cases and no class D “non-identified” case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://carteovni.fr/region/auvergne-rhone-alpes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carteovni.fr">[CarteOvni.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carteovni.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Carte Ovni.fr OVN I en Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNI en Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes - CarteOvni.frObservations d&#x27;OVNI et phénomènes aérospatiaux non identifiés en Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes: cas du...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Explained Cases" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The mechanisms are revealing. A red glow became a probable distress flare at Beaune-d’Allier. A “stationary” white sphere over Monetay-sur-Allier became a gas balloon from the Gordon Bennett Cup. Red points and moving lights at Nizerolles became wind turbine lights, Jupiter, public lighting and Christmas decorations. At Moulins, a ten-second brightening point matched an Iridium satellite flare; at Arronnes, a white point matched the International Space Station; at Bézenet, two flashing lights matched military aircraft. These cases matter because they show the same pattern again and again: Allier’s strongest UFO evidence often points towards misidentification, not towards exotic craft.

## Why “explained” does not mean “nothing happened”

An explained UFO case is not a hoax by default. In many Allier reports, witnesses noticed real lights, real objects or real astronomical events, then interpreted them under difficult viewing conditions. GEIPAN’s own classification system is built around two ideas: “strangeness”, meaning how far the report remains from known causes after investigation, and “consistency”, meaning how much reliable information is available. Class A means the observation was explained without ambiguity; class B means GEIPAN regards the retained explanation as very probable; class C means the observation cannot be analysed for lack of information; class D means unexplained despite the available evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/glossaire" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

That distinction is essential for Allier. The department’s explained cases are useful because they preserve the human puzzle while reducing the mystery. Witnesses saw something; the question is whether the observation still resists known causes after the basic checks. In Allier, many reports stopped being puzzling when investigators could compare the sighting with celestial maps, satellite predictions, balloon tracks, photographs, aircraft behaviour or local lighting.

The result is a department-level pattern. Allier has rural horizons, small towns, open skies, scattered lighting and occasional aviation or balloon activity. Those conditions are ideal for honest mistakes: a low Moon can look like a red bar near a hedge; Venus can seem to hover; a satellite flare can appear and vanish in seconds; a balloon can seem motionless when the viewer lacks distance clues.<img src="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Explained Cases illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Lasers, flares and aircraft

Allier’s most practical lesson is that artificial lights do not need to behave like ordinary aircraft to be ordinary. Searchlights, distress flares, aircraft strobes and lanterns can all appear silent, oddly coloured, discontinuous or detached from an obvious source.

The Gannat case from 6 October 1988 is a simple example. GEIPAN classed it A after identifying the observation as a hypermarket laser projector. The case is brief, but it matters because it represents a common UFO trap: a light beam or projected effect can be seen at a distance without the witness seeing the equipment that produces it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-10-01149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Beaune-d’Allier, on 30 April 1990, is more dramatic. Witnesses described a very bright red phenomenon moving silently on a descending path, with illumination of woodland below it. GEIPAN classed the case B, not A, and identified the likely cause as a distress flare. The “B” matters: this was not presented as mathematically certain, but as a very probable explanation after re-examination. The case had three testimonies and was updated again in the public record in 2026, which shows how older reports can be revisited rather than simply left in their original state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/50247?field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=12&amp;page=%2C169" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Bézenet, on 5 July 2022, shows the modern aircraft version of the same problem. A witness saw two luminous points with harsh white stroboscopic flashes moving from west to east. GEIPAN classed the report A and identified it as a patrol of military aircraft returning from mission. Its explanation is persuasive because the described lights matched anti-collision strobes, and the witness also perceived a slight aircraft-like noise, even though the apparent movement did not feel quite like the sound source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/61104?field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_date_d_observation&amp;page=10&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

## Planets, satellites and the Moon

Some of the strongest Allier explanations come from the sky itself. Astronomical and satellite cases are useful because they can often be checked after the event: if a witness gives a time, direction and position, investigators can compare the report with known planetary positions, Moon rise, satellite passes or flare predictions.

Monetay-sur-Allier on 5 December 1998 is one of the clearest examples because the case was once more mysterious than it now appears. GEIPAN describes observations of a vivid red luminous bar on the eastern horizon, with an impression that it advanced and retreated towards the witnesses. After re-examination, the case was classed A as a Moonrise misidentification. The public GEIPAN note says the witness was contacted by telephone, confirmed the exact viewing position and hedge heights, and accepted the mechanism described by investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1998-12-01714" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

That case is valuable because it shows why a Moon explanation can feel unsatisfying at first. The Moon is familiar, but near the horizon it can be reddened, partly hidden, enlarged by comparison with foreground objects and apparently mobile when clouds, hedges or the witness’s own movement alter the frame of reference. GEIPAN’s 2017 note on revisited cases also lists Monetay-sur-Allier among older cases reclassified with the hypothesis “red Moon”, showing that later re-analysis can turn a former puzzle into an identified case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/actualites/geipan-revisite-cas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">geipan revisite cas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">geipan revisite cas</span></span></span>

Domérat, on 27 November 2013, illustrates the planetary version. The witness reported a discontinuous luminous phenomenon in a clear sky. GEIPAN classed the case A: Venus explained the end of the observation, while aircraft or helicopters probably explained the beginning. This mixed explanation is important. Real UFO reports often combine more than one stimulus, and a witness may honestly join separate lights into one perceived event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/56406?field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=11&amp;order=title&amp;page=%2C82&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Nizerolles, on 26 December 2014, is even more layered. Two witnesses reported several points of light in the night sky. GEIPAN classed the case A and concluded that the reported phenomena were very probably a combination of wind turbine warning lights, the planet Jupiter, public lighting, Christmas decorations and, for one witness, a physiological eye-movement effect described as micro-nystagmus. The case is a good antidote to the idea that every sighting needs one single cause. Sometimes the answer is a stack of ordinary causes seen from one location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2014-12-09167" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Moulins, on 29 August 2015, is a compact satellite case. A witness saw a point of light in the north-west brighten and then fade over about ten seconds. GEIPAN identified the event as an Iridium flare from Iridium 54, with a predicted maximum magnitude of -6.6, and classed the case A. The short duration, star-like point, brightening and fading pattern all matched the known satellite-reflection mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/57498?customGetLattitude=46.94358292648825&amp;customGetLongitude=4.4989013671875&amp;customGetZoom=7&amp;field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=49.11702904077932&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=44.77013681219717&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=7.668457031250001&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=1.329345703125&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=57&amp;sort=desc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Arronnes, on 31 May 2019, shows how the International Space Station can enter the UFO record. The witness saw a single white point in a clear night sky; GEIPAN classed the case A and recorded the phenomenon as the ISS. The case’s low strangeness score fits a typical ISS pass: a bright, silent, steady point crossing the sky without aircraft navigation lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/58361" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Explained Cases illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Balloons, photographs and mistaken scale

Balloons are especially important in Allier because they expose a weakness in ordinary perception: without a known distance, the eye struggles to judge size, speed and altitude. A small nearby object can look like a large distant one, and a large distant object can look almost stationary.

The Monetay-sur-Allier case from 2 September 2012 is the best example. During a family gathering, a witness saw a high, white, spherical object that seemed nearly stationary. Two photographs were taken, which made the case more analysable than many single-witness reports. GEIPAN classed it A after linking the observation to a gas balloon from the 56th Gordon Bennett Cup, whose crews had launched from Switzerland and whose tracks brought several balloons across central France.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-09-09385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

This case is particularly instructive because the photographs did not make the event more mysterious; they helped make it less mysterious. GEIPAN’s account says the images were analysed with IPACO, its preferred image and video analysis software, and the investigation considered the observation parameters, the balloon race and the wider experience GEIPAN had with similar cases. A white sphere that seemed to hover became a trackable aerial object once timing and external records were added.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/2012-09-09385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Lurcy-Lévis, on 25 August 2019, is listed by GEIPAN as a class A balloon case, and is also cited in a specialist French sky-misidentification resource under solar balloons. Solar balloons can look particularly odd because they use sunlight rather than a burner, can be quiet, and may drift rather than fly like an aircraft. In rural Allier, that makes them plausible candidates for reports of slow or silent objects without obvious propulsion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/recherche/cas/tab?field_agregation_index_value=&amp;field_classification_des_cas_target_id%5B0%5D=11&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_date_d_observation_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_date_value=&amp;field_departement_target_id=&amp;field_document_existe_ou_pas_value=All&amp;field_is_new_value=All&amp;field_is_revisited_value=All&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_latitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmax%5D=&amp;field_longitude_value%5Bmin%5D=&amp;field_phenomene_target_id=&amp;field_type_de_cas_target_id=All&amp;order=field_departement_textuel&amp;page=%2C90&amp;sort=asc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

A picture may preserve shape and brightness, but without distance, lens data, direction, timing and comparison objects, it may not prove scale. ## What these explanations reveal about Allier’s UFO history

The explained Allier cases do not make the department uninteresting. They make it unusually useful. Instead of a famous unsolved set-piece, Allier offers a spread of ordinary mechanisms across different decades: lasers in 1988, a probable flare in 1990, the Moon in 1998, lanterns and balloons in 2012, Venus in 2013, Jupiter and local lights in 2014, an Iridium flare in 2015, the ISS and balloons in 2019, and military aircraft in 2022.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1988-10-01149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan+7cnes-geipan.fr+7Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Three patterns stand out.

First, **direction and timing are often decisive**. Moulins depended on the exact 21:26 time and north-west direction matching an Iridium flare. Domérat depended on matching the end of the observation with Venus and the earlier part with likely aircraft. Arronnes depended on the timing and track of the ISS. Without those details, the same reports would sound much stranger.

Second, **a single sighting may contain multiple ordinary stimuli**. Nizerolles is the clearest case: turbine lights, Jupiter, local lighting, Christmas decorations and eye movement all contributed to the final interpretation. Domérat also combined Venus with probable aircraft or helicopters. This matters because many UFO debates assume a report must have one clean explanation. Allier’s explained cases show that real perception is messier.

Third, **older cases can change status when re-examined**. Monetay-sur-Allier’s 1998 Moon case is the best local example. It was part of a batch of older GEIPAN cases revisited with improved tools and experience, and the public file says the witness later confirmed details that supported the Moonrise explanation. This is not debunking by dismissal; it is reclassification by reconstruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes-geipan.fr/fr/cas/1998-12-01714" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Explained Cases illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/Allier_716dd6_explained_cases_65b034-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## How to read an Allier report before calling it mysterious

The explained cases suggest a practical reading method for Allier’s wider UFO file. A report is stronger when it contains exact time, viewing direction, duration, weather, witness position, photographs or video with context, independent witnesses, and checks against known aircraft, balloons, satellites and astronomical bodies. A report is weaker when it offers only a vague light, no precise direction, no timing, no environmental context and no way to test competing explanations.

That is why GEIPAN’s class C category should not be treated as a hidden reservoir of strong mysteries. GEIPAN states that class C cases are not analysable for lack of information, while class D cases remain unexplained despite the information available. In a department like Allier, where the public record contains many A cases and no listed D cases in the CarteOvni departmental summary, this distinction changes the whole tone of the local UFO history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/fr/node/422" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Allier’s explained cases are therefore not a side note to the department’s UFO story. They are the main interpretive key.
